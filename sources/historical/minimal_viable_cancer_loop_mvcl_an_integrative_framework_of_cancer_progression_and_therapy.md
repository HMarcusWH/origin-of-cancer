Minimal Viable Cancer Loop (MVCL): An
Integrative Framework of Cancer Progression and
Therapy

Abstract

Cancer progresses through a self-sustaining cycle of variation, selection, and adaptation. We propose the
Minimal Viable Cancer Loop (MVCL) as a unifying framework, wherein nascent oncogenic events (“Seeds”)
are nurtured by permissive microenvironments (“Sinks”), undergo critical malignant transitions (“Switches”),
and expand into progressing tumors (“Spread”). The end-stage tumor then feeds back by generating new
genetic diversity and remodeling the microenvironment, perpetuating the loop. This integrative model
connects  traditionally  siloed aspects  of cancer biology – from genomic  instability and  epigenetic
reprogramming to immune evasion and therapy resistance – into a continuous process. We synthesize
evidence across 13 key biological modules that align with MVCL phases, highlighting each module’s causal
chain, universal principles, context-specific features, clinical biomarkers, therapeutic vulnerabilities, and a
falsifiable prediction. Recent advances (2023–2025) have largely upheld the MVCL: for example, ATR/CHK1
inhibitors showed benefit in replication stress-high tumors, supporting the DNA damage module, and
extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA)-driven tumors responded to targeted therapies, validating the structural
variation module. Adaptive therapy trials delayed resistance in prostate cancer (progression-free survival
33.5 vs 14.3 months)  1    2  , demonstrating that evolutionary-informed dosing can improve outcomes. No
core module has been falsified to date; however, some remain to be proven (e.g. stable re-differentiation of
advanced tumors has not been achieved). Overall, the MVCL provides a cohesive, testable framework that
captures how disparate cancer processes cooperate to drive malignancy.  It  offers a roadmap  for
understanding tumor evolution and for designing multi-pronged interventions to disrupt the cancer loop at
its vulnerable nodes.

Introduction

Cancer arises from an evolutionary process in which cells accumulate heritable changes and undergo
selection over time  3    4  . Early models such as the Hallmarks of Cancer described distinct capabilities
that tumor cells acquire (e.g. sustaining proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell
death)  5  . However, those paradigms considered each hallmark in isolation  6  . Here we adopt an
integrative perspective that views cancer as a dynamic, self-perpetuating system. We introduce the Minimal
Viable Cancer Loop (MVCL), which conceptualizes tumorigenesis and progression as a cyclic process with
four interlinked phases: Seed, Sink, Switch, and Spread. In this model, nascent oncogenic alterations (the
“Seed”) are fostered by permissive tissue niches (the “Sink”), cross critical malignant thresholds (“Switch”),
and expand under ecological and evolutionary forces (“Spread”). Crucially, the end-stage tumor feeds back:
ongoing genomic instability continually generates new mutant seeds, and tumor–stromal interactions
deepen the supportive sinks  7    8  . This creates a malignant feedback loop wherein each cycle reinforces
the next  9   10  .

By breaking any link in this loop, one could theoretically stall or regress cancer progression  11   12  . The
MVCL framework thus highlights points of vulnerability for intervention at multiple stages of cancer
development. We aim to provide a cohesive, falsifiable model that connects diverse aspects of cancer
biology into a single system. In doing so, we integrate evidence across 13 major “modules” of cancer – from
genomic instability and epigenetics to immune evasion and therapy resistance – and illustrate how they
collectively drive an evolving tumor. Each module corresponds to well-established cancer hallmarks or
enabling characteristics  13   8  , but MVCL emphasizes their interdependence and ordering within an
ongoing cycle. This approach builds on decades of cancer research: from Nowell’s clonal evolution theory
  14 and the Hallmarks framework  5  to newer insights like the pre-metastatic niche and immunoediting.
By situating each process in a loop, we can better understand how early events enable later ones, how
tumors maintain themselves, and why certain combinations of traits are so dangerous. The MVCL is meant
to be falsifiable – it generates specific predictions (e.g. targeting a given module will disrupt the loop and
yield therapeutic benefit, or particular experimental outcomes would invalidate a link). In the following
sections, we outline the methods used to construct the MVCL and then detail each module, including its
mechanistic role, key evidence, and a pivotal falsifier. Finally, we discuss how these modules integrate and
consider implications for therapy.

Methods and Framework

MVCL Construction: We constructed the MVCL by distilling causal “backbone” pathways from major
domains of cancer biology and arranging them into a continuous loop of four stages: Seed, Sink, Switch,
and Spread. Each stage represents a conceptual step in cancer development. Seed encompasses sources of
heritable variation and the initial breakdown of homeostatic controls. Sink represents the selective tumor
microenvironments and systemic factors that favor malignant growth. Switch denotes the transition points
where cells enter a self-perpetuating malignant program (e.g. bypassing failsafe mechanisms or adopting
new phenotypes). Spread covers the expansion, invasion, and remodeling activities of advanced tumors. We
aligned 13 key biological modules to these phases based on their primary roles (Table 1). Notably, many
modules span multiple phases (for instance, a process may contribute to both an initial seed and a later
switch); in such cases we assigned the module to all relevant phases.

Literature Synthesis: For each module, we performed a comprehensive review of peer-reviewed studies
(with emphasis on 2010–2025) to map out: (a) the module’s mechanistic chain of events, (b) invariant
principles observed across many cancers vs. context-specific differences, (c) clinically relevant diagnostics or
biomarkers,  (d) current and emerging therapeutic interventions (the “control levers” that target the
module), (e) interfaces with other modules (i.e. inputs from or outputs to other cancer processes), and (f) a
key falsifiable proposition – a testable uncertainty whose outcome could invalidate or substantially revise
the module’s role in the MVCL. Each link in the MVCL loop is supported by evidence from high-impact
studies, cited throughout, to ensure the framework remains evidence-based and not merely theoretical.

Scope and Terminology: The MVCL is intended as an integrative hypothesis for solid tumors (though many
concepts apply to hematologic malignancies as well). We use “module” to refer to a cluster of related
biological processes (often corresponding to a classic cancer hallmark or enabling characteristic). For
example, “Somatic mutation & clonal evolution” is one module encompassing mutagenesis and clonal
selection, while “Tumor microenvironment” is another encompassing hypoxia, stromal support, etc. By
“minimal viable” loop, we mean the smallest set of interconnected modules sufficient to drive sustained
malignant growth – recognizing that real tumors may have additional complexities. We also introduce some

1. Somatic Mutation & Clonal Evolution (Seed/Spread)

MVCL Role: Seed & Spread. This module provides the heritable genomic diversity that initiates tumors (the
“seed”) and drives ongoing subclonal evolution during tumor expansion (the “spread”)  3    4  . In essence,
successive mutations generate a heterogeneous population of cells on which selection can act  8  . Clonal
evolution underlies how cancers adapt and become more aggressive over time  15  .

Causal Chain: Endogenous and exogenous mutational processes create a continuous supply of genetic
variants. Sources of DNA mutations include spontaneous errors in DNA replication and repair, as well as
environmental carcinogens (e.g. UV light, tobacco carcinogens) and enzymatic processes like APOBEC
cytidine deamination  16  . Early driver mutations (for instance, activating KRAS or inactivating TP53) confer
growth or survival advantages to cells  9  . These mutant clones expand locally, but also face constraints
from the tissue microenvironment and immune system, which act as filters. Additional selective pressures
come from therapy: when a patient is treated with a drug, sensitive clones are eliminated, allowing resistant
clones (if present) to dominate (this is an accelerated replay of natural selection)  12   17  . Genomic instability
mechanisms – such as mismatch repair defects, abnormally active mutator enzymes, or chromosomal
instability – further fuel mutation generation throughout the tumor’s life  8   18  . The result is a branched
evolutionary tree of subclones within a single cancer, with competition and selection determining which
subclones rise to dominance  8  . This clonal heterogeneity provides the substrate for adaptation to new
challenges (e.g. therapy, metastatic environments)  12  .

Invariants & Context: A universal principle is the interplay of genetic diversity plus selection in cancer
evolution  19  . Across virtually all cancers, a set of common pathways tend to be mutated (“mountains” in
genomic landscapes) – for example, the RTK/RAS/PI3K signaling pathway, cell cycle regulators like RB, or
DNA repair genes  19  . These reflect recurring evolutionary solutions that cancers arrive at independently
  20   21  . Another invariant is intratumoral heterogeneity itself: sequencing studies find that even early-
stage tumors harbor multiple subclones with distinct mutations  22  . However, there are context-dependent
differences. Some tumors have hypermutated genomes (e.g. microsatellite-unstable or POLE-mutant
tumors with tens of thousands of mutations) versus “quiet” genomes (pediatric cancers or certain leukemias
with few mutations)  22  . The spectrum of mutations also varies: for instance, UV-induced skin cancers show
a predominance of C->T transitions at dipyrimidine sites, whereas lung cancers in smokers have a high load
of G->T transversions from tobacco carcinogens  10  . Pediatric tumors often have fewer point mutations but
may rely more on structural variations or epigenetic changes. Additionally, evolutionary patterns can differ
– some tumors show branching evolution with many coexisting subclones, while others are almost
monoclonal expansions of one dominant clone (often due to a particularly advantageous driver). Despite
these differences, the principle that mutation + selection drives progression holds broadly  19   23  .
Notably, neutral evolution (where many mutations hitchhike without strong selection) has been proposed to
dominate in some cancers, but even there, selection becomes apparent under therapeutic pressure  24  .
This balance between deterministic selection and stochastic drift is an area of active research  24  .

Diagnostics/Biomarkers: The genetic makeup of a tumor can be profiled by sequencing technologies. Bulk
whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing reveals the mutation profile and clonal architecture (variant
allele frequencies can be used to infer subclones and their frequencies)  25   26  . Single-cell sequencing can
resolve heterogeneity with even finer granularity, directly observing subclone genotypes. Mutational
signatures (patterns of mutations across the genome) can identify the dominant mutagenic processes (e.g.
a UV signature, an APOBEC signature)  25   26  . Tracking mutations over time – for example, via serial liquid
biopsies detecting circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) – can reveal clonal evolution and emerging resistant
clones  27  . Phylogenetic analysis of multi-region sequencing (sampling different parts of the tumor or
primary vs metastasis) shows the evolutionary relationships between subclones  26  . Clinically, tests like
tumor mutation burden (TMB) are used as biomarkers  (high TMB often  correlates with  better
immunotherapy response, as it implies more neoantigens)  28   29  . Another example: monitoring specific
resistance mutations (e.g. EGFR T790M in lung cancer) in blood can guide therapy switches  30   31  . In
summary, genomic profiling is central to both diagnosis (identifying driver mutations for targeted therapy)
and tracking disease evolution.

Control Levers: Targeting this module means exploiting the tumor’s reliance on certain mutations or its
vulnerability due to acquired mutations. Standard interventions include early detection and surgical
removal of neoplastic lesions before they diversify further (e.g. resecting a pre-malignant polyp). Adjuvant
therapies (chemo or radiation after surgery) are timed to eliminate minimal residual disease, aiming to wipe
out small subclones before they evolve resistance  32  . In metastatic settings, targeted therapies against
oncogenic drivers (e.g. EGFR inhibitors in EGFR-mutant lung cancer, BRAF inhibitors in BRAF-mutant
melanoma) can induce dramatic initial responses – an illustration of “oncogene addiction” where the cancer
is critically dependent on a single mutated gene  33   20  . However, resistance eventually emerges in most
cases due to clonal evolution  17   34  . To delay resistance, adaptive therapy or intermittent dosing
strategies have been proposed  35  . These aim to maintain a stable tumor burden by not eliminating all
sensitive cells – thereby keeping competitive pressure on resistant cells  36   1  . The recent pilot trial in
prostate cancer, where abiraterone was cycled on/off based on PSA, showed significantly prolonged time to
progression and overall survival with such an evolution-informed approach  1    2  . Combination therapies
that target multiple subclones or evolutionary pathways at once are another approach:  e.g. using
combination EGFR plus MET inhibitors in lung cancer to preempt the common MET-amplification resistance
pathway, or combining BRAF and MEK inhibitors in melanoma to reduce escape routes  37   38  . Emerging
strategies include targeting tumor-specific neoantigens with vaccines or T cells (which effectively target the
product of unique mutations), and employing mutagenic agents or hyperthermia to drive tumors into an
“evolutionary dead end” (forcing excessive mutation load that leads to non-viable progeny, though this is
experimental). Another lever is exploiting fitness costs of resistance: if a resistant clone has a weakness (e.g.
a drug-resistant clone relying on an alternate pathway), a second drug can target that weakness (this is
sometimes called collateral sensitivity). For instance, loss of PTEN (conferring resistance to some targeted
therapies) creates a dependency on the PI3K/AKT pathway which can itself be targeted  39   40  . In summary,
while we cannot “stop” mutations from occurring, we can leverage knowledge of key driver mutations (with
targeted drugs) and evolutionary principles (with dosing strategies and combinations) to manage tumor
evolution.

Interfaces: This module feeds into and is fed by others. Inputs: Environmental and lifestyle factors that
increase mutation rates (tobacco smoke, UV exposure, certain diets or inflammation) funnel into this
module by creating more seeds  18  . Inherited DNA repair defects (e.g. BRCA1/2 mutations or Lynch
syndrome) also feed in by elevating baseline mutagenesis. Outputs: The mutations generated here fuel
downstream modules. Notably, mutations  in  cell-cycle or checkpoint genes lead to chromosomal
instability (CIN) – an “error snowball” where genomic instability begets more instability  41   42  . Mutations
in surface proteins or creation of neoantigens affect the immune evasion module by shaping the
neoantigen landscape – highly mutated tumors present many new antigens that immune  cells can
potentially recognize  41  . Some mutations directly upregulate immune-inhibitory signals (e.g. PTEN loss can
lead to immune-evasive cytokine profiles  43   44  ). Additionally, the microbiome can influence mutation
processes: for example, bacteria can produce reactive species or metabolites that induce DNA damage
(colorectal cancer has a mutational signature linked to Pks+ E.  coli), and chronic inflammation from
dysbiosis can increase turnover and mutation in epithelial cells  18  . Conversely, outputs of this module
strongly affect Therapy: the specific driver mutations a tumor has will determine what systemic therapies
are effective (tying into the Systemic Therapy/Resistance module). For example, a tumor’s EGFR mutation
status guides use of EGFR inhibitors, BRAF status guides use of BRAF/MEK inhibitors, etc. Thus, this module’s
output essentially sets the initial conditions for therapeutic intervention.  It also feeds the Structural
Variation module in that point mutations in mitotic checkpoint genes (like TP53 or ATM) can lead to
chromosomal breakage and structural alterations  41  .

Key Falsifier: Neutral-drift predominance. The MVCL assumes that selection plays a major role in driving
clonal evolution (especially under therapy). A falsifying scenario would be if most tumor evolution were
neutral. For instance, if high-resolution longitudinal sequencing showed that tumor subclones rise and fall
in frequency predominantly by random drift rather than selective advantage, then the importance of
Darwinian selection (and by extension, adaptive therapy’s premise) would be overestimated  24  . In such a
case, many subclonal mutations would be “hitchhikers” with no functional impact, and stopping treatment
intermittently (adaptive therapy) might not meaningfully delay resistance because resistant clones might
emerge stochastically. Evidence to test this includes fitting mathematical models to tumor phylogenies:
some studies in untreated cancers have reported that neutral models fit subclonal mutation frequency
distributions surprisingly well  45  . However, under therapy, strong selective sweeps are commonly observed
(e.g. emergence of EGFR T790M clones after EGFR inhibitors)  17   34  . If it were found that even with therapy,
subclonal dynamics were mostly neutral (e.g. resistant clones present at progression were actually already
randomly drifting at high frequency before therapy), it would challenge the emphasis on selection. So far,
data indicate that while some neutral drift occurs, therapy clearly exerts selection pressure in most cases
  17   34  . Thus, this falsifier has not been met – adaptive therapy success suggests selection is indeed a
major force  1    2  .

2. DNA Damage Response & Replication Stress (Seed/Switch)

MVCL Role: Seed & Switch. Defects in the DNA damage response (DDR) and the presence of replication
stress contribute to early genomic variation (seed) and also act as a pivotal switch by enabling a cell to
enter a mutator state that accelerates malignant transformation  46   47  . In normal cells, robust DNA repair
and cell cycle checkpoints prevent the accumulation of mutations. When these safeguards fail – through
mutations in DDR genes or overwhelming replication stress – cells can tolerate a higher mutation load and
genomic instability, which propels them toward malignancy  48   49  . Thus, the transition from a benign to a
malignant state often involves crossing a threshold of genomic instability (a switch) where error correction
fails.

Causal Chain: Oncogene activation (e.g. MYC, RAS) or tumor suppressor loss can drive cells into rapid
proliferation, causing replication stress – a state where DNA replication is impeded or incomplete at many
forks  50   51  .  Replication  stress  arises from  factors  like  insufficient  nucleotides,  collisions between
replication and transcription machinery, or  difficult-to-replicate genomic regions.  It leads to  stalled
replication forks (accumulation of single-stranded DNA)  52  . If forks collapse, they create DNA double-
strand breaks, which activate the ATR–CHK1–WEE1 checkpoint pathways in normal cells to pause the cycle
and repair  52  . A cell experiencing chronic replication stress will rely heavily on the DDR to survive.
Meanwhile, inherited or acquired defects in DDR genes (e.g. mutations in BRCA1/2, ATM, p53, or mismatch
repair genes) undermine the repair of DNA lesions  53   54  . When repair fails, DNA damage accumulates,
leading to mutations and larger-scale genomic changes. In early tumorigenesis, this process is paradoxical:
a cell must survive initial oncogenic stress (by disabling apoptosis via p53 loss, for instance) yet allow just
enough DNA damage to generate variation  55   56  . Commonly, incipient cancer cells achieve this by
attenuating but not abolishing the DDR – e.g. losing the G1/S checkpoint (p53) while retaining some G2/M
arrest through ATR/CHK1, which lets them accumulate mutations but avoid immediate mitotic catastrophe.
As a result, these cells enter a “mutator phenotype” state  55  first described by Loeb, where their mutation
rate is elevated. This dramatically increases the chance of acquiring additional driver mutations (the seed
for further evolution). Thus, replication stress and DDR failure work in tandem: replication stress provides a
constant source of DNA lesions, and DDR defects ensure those lesions often go unrepaired, converting into
permanent mutations or structural aberrations  47   51  . A key outcome is chromosomal instability (CIN) –

Invariants & Context:  It  is nearly universal that cancers exhibit some form of DDR impairment or
replication stress. For example, high levels of replication stress markers (like RPA-coated single-strand DNA,

in breast/ovarian/prostate cancers, mismatch repair defects in colorectal and others, p53 pathway defects
in over half of all cancers  60   61  . These are invariant themes – genomic instability is a hallmark of
cancer  62   60  . Context-specific differences include the type of instability: tumors with MSI (microsatellite
instability) due to MMR deficiency have lots of small insertion/deletion mutations, whereas tumors with
chromosomal instability (CIN) due to misrepair have aneuploidy and structural variants. Hypoxia in the
tumor microenvironment can exacerbate replication stress by causing dNTP imbalances and fork stalling
  50   63  ; thus, tumors growing faster than their blood supply often face extra replication stress. Another
context switch: some cancers (e.g. testicular germ cell tumors) retain wild-type p53 and are very sensitive to
DNA damage (hence curable with chemo), whereas others (e.g. pancreatic cancer) have mutated p53 and
robust DNA damage tolerance, making them chemo-resistant. The presence of viral oncogenes (like HPV
E6/E7) in cervical cancer disables p53 and Rb, leading to unique patterns of genomic instability. In summary,
while mechanisms vary, the presence of replication stress and some DDR aberration is an invariant feature
across cancers  47  . An important invariant concept is synthetic lethality – cancer cells often survive by
compensatory pathways (e.g. a BRCA-deficient cell relies on PARP-mediated repair), which can be targeted.

Diagnostics/Biomarkers: Several biomarkers assess replication stress and DDR status. A classical marker is
nuclear foci of γH2AX (phosphorylated H2AX) indicating DNA double-strand breaks; multiple γH2AX foci per
nucleus are often seen in tumor cells and can be detected by immunohistochemistry  59   64  . Coating of
single-stranded DNA by RPA protein  is a marker of replication stress (detectable in  cell assays)  59  .
Phosphorylated CHK1 or ATR indicates active checkpoint response to stress  59  . In terms of genomic
measures, the HRD score (homologous recombination deficiency score) is used clinically: it quantifies the
“scarring” pattern in the genome (large-scale state transitions, loss of heterozygosity, telomeric allelic
imbalance) to infer BRCA-like HR repair defects  65  . A high HRD score predicts sensitivity to PARP inhibitors,
for instance. Microsatellite instability (MSI) testing via PCR or IHC for mismatch repair proteins is a routine
diagnostic for certain cancers (e.g. colon, endometrial)  66  . Mutational signature analysis can reveal MMR
deficiency (Signature 6/15) or APOBEC activity (Signature 2/13), etc., which speak to specific DDR contexts.
Functional assays, though mostly in research, include RAD51 foci formation (to test HR competency: HR-
proficient cells form RAD51 foci at damage sites). Liquid biopsy approaches like ctDNA copy-number
instability (CNI) scores measure fragmentation patterns of tumor DNA in blood, which correlate with
chromosomal instability  67   68  . Overall, detecting a DDR defect has clinical importance: for example,
BRCA1/2 or other HR gene mutations in a tumor are tested to guide use of PARP inhibitors  65   69  , and MSI-
high status is an indication for immunotherapy (because of high neoantigen load)  70  .

suggest that blocking ATR is not actually hitting a cancer-specific Achilles’ heel. This could happen if, say,
normal tissues also suffer toxicity (narrow therapeutic window) or if tumors quickly bypass ATR dependence.
Such a result would force a revision of how we exploit replication stress – perhaps needing combination
approaches or different biomarkers. Early data have been promising (partial responses in ATM-deficient
tumors with ATR inhibitor, etc.), but definitive Phase III evidence is pending. Another falsifier would be if
tumors could compensate for ATR loss by unknown mechanisms, making ATR inhibitors broadly ineffective.
To date, the falsifier has not been realized: multiple trials have reported objective responses in replication-
stress-high cancers  72   73  , supporting the concept. Nonetheless, one  trial failure could reshape the
strategy. In summary, the success of drugs like ATR/CHK1 inhibitors is an ongoing test of the idea that
pushing already stressed cancer cells over the edge will selectively kill them.

3. Structural Variation & Extrachromosomal DNA (Seed/Spread)

MVCL Role: Seed & Spread. Large-scale genome rearrangements – including chromosomal structural
variations (SVs) and extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) – provide high-impact genetic variation that seeds
malignant traits and fuels tumor evolution during spread  86   87  . Unlike point mutations, which are single
nucleotide changes,  structural  variations can  amplify oncogenes  to extreme  levels,  delete tumor
suppressors, create novel gene fusions, or spawn circular ecDNA particles carrying multiple gene copies.
These events can give rise to aggressive subclones and heterogeneity that drives adaptation and metastasis
  88   89  . Thus, structural alterations in the genome are a powerful engine for cancer’s “leaps” in evolution.

Causal Chain: Structural variations often originate from DNA breaks and misrepair. Sources include the
replication stress and DNA damage discussed in Module 2 (collapsed forks, mis-repaired double-strand
breaks), as well as mitotic errors (improper segregation can shear chromosomes). A single catastrophic
event like chromothripsis (shattering of a chromosome and haphazard rejoining) can produce dozens of
rearrangements at once  78  . Mechanisms  like the breakage-fusion-bridge (BFB) cycle – triggered by
telomere  crisis  –  generate  amplifications and  deletions  through  iterative  breakage  of  dicentric
chromosomes  90   91  . When segments of chromosomes are rejoined incorrectly, they yield translocations
(which  can form  fusion oncogenes  like  BCR-ABL),  deletions  (knocking  out tumor  suppressors),
duplications, or inversions. Particularly potent are gene amplifications: if a proto-oncogene is placed in
an environment where  it can be copied many times,  its expression skyrockets. Two major routes of
amplification in cancer are (a) linear amplifications – tandem duplications on chromosomes, sometimes
visible as homogeneous staining regions (HSRs) – and (b) circular extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) – where a
piece of a chromosome containing one or several oncogenes is excised and forms a mini-chromosome that
replicates independently  92   93  . ecDNA elements (also historically called double minutes) are not tethered

4. Epigenetic Reprogramming & Lineage Plasticity (Seed/Switch)

MVCL Role: Seed & Switch. Epigenetic dysregulation provides a source of non-genetic variability that can
initiate tumors (by altering gene expression programs without altering DNA sequence – a “seed” of
transformation) and drives phenotypic switches to more malignant cell states (such as transitions to stem-
like or drug-resistant cell identities) 120  121 . In other words, cancer cells often escape normal cell fate
controls and adopt new identities through epigenetic reprogramming, enabling them to cross critical
malignant thresholds (“switches”) such as unlimited self-renewal or transdifferentiation under therapy
pressure.

Causal Chain: Early in tumorigenesis, widespread changes in the epigenome occur. DNA methylation
patterns become aberrant: global hypomethylation leads to genomic  instability and  activation  of
transposable  elements,  while  focal  hypermethylation  silences  key tumor  suppressor genes  (e.g.
hypermethylation of the CDKN2A promoter silences p16, or of MLH1 silences DNA repair)  62  122 . Histone
modifications are also disrupted: the balance of activating marks (like H3K27ac) and repressive marks (like
H3K27me3) shifts across the genome  62  122 . Mutations in chromatin regulators (e.g. ARID1A, a SWI/SNF
complex member, or EZH2 in the PRC2 complex) frequently occur and contribute to these epigenetic
changes 123  124 . The result is that normal differentiation programs are upset: genes that should be off can
become aberrantly active and vice versa. This yields cells in a “plastic” or stem-like state with high
transcriptional noise and multilineage potential 125  126 . For example, epigenetic activation of embryonic
transcription factors like OCT4, SOX2, or c-Myc can endow differentiated cells with stem-like traits and
immortality 127  128 . Similarly, polycomb repressor activation (via EZH2) can silence differentiation genes,
locking cells in an undifferentiated state 127  128 . As tumors progress, this plasticity enables lineage
switching: under stress (like treatment or a new microenvironment), cancer cells can alter their phenotype
to one better suited to survive. Classic examples: prostate adenocarcinoma (which is androgen receptor
[AR]-driven) can, after potent AR pathway inhibition, switch to a neuroendocrine phenotype that no longer
depends on AR 121  129 . This involves epigenetic reprogramming – turning off AR-regulated epithelial genes
and turning on neuronal genes, often requiring loss of p53/Rb and activation of factors like SOX2 130  131 .
Another example is small-cell lung cancer transformation from an EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma
treated with EGFR inhibitors – a histologic switch that involves widespread chromatin changes. These
transitions don’t come from new driver mutations (often the genome is the same) but from epigenetic
plasticity. Epigenetics also ties into metastasis: epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is largely an
epigenetically governed process where epithelial cell adhesion genes are silenced and mesenchymal genes
are activated (via factors like TWIST, SNAIL) 121  129 . This confers migratory ability – an important switch.
Importantly, epigenetic changes are potentially reversible (unlike mutations) – cells maintain the machinery
to re-establish certain patterns, which is why epigenetic drugs can sometimes re-differentiate tumors. For
instance, DNA demethylating agents or HDAC inhibitors can induce expression of silenced genes (like
reactivating fetal hemoglobin in some anemias). In cancer therapy, low-dose DNA methyltransferase
inhibitors have been shown to induce a more differentiated, less malignant state in some tumors 132  133 .
However, stable re-differentiation of aggressive cancers has been elusive – often, after drug removal, cells
revert (hence “non-durable reprogramming” is a concern). Lastly, epigenetic changes can cause immune
evasion: e.g. hypermethylation of antigen presentation genes (TAP1, HLA class I) can allow tumors to hide
from T cells 134  135 . Conversely, epigenetic drugs can upregulate these genes (there’s synergy between
azacitidine/HDAC inhibitors and PD-1 inhibitors via a “viral mimicry” effect where reactivated transposons
induce interferon) 135  136 .

5. Oncogene & Tumor Suppressor Circuitry (and Non-Oncogene Dependencies) (Switch)

MVCL Role: Switch (and Seed). The activation of dominant oncogenes and inactivation of key tumor
suppressors represent a fundamental switch to a malignant state 165  166 . These genetic alterations drive
unchecked cell proliferation and survival (hallmark traits) and also contribute to initial clonal expansion (the
seed of the tumor) by conferring growth advantages 167  168 . Additionally, they rewire core cellular circuits
and create new stress phenotypes, making the cancer cell reliant on certain housekeeping pathways (“non-
oncogene dependencies”) to cope 169  170 .

Causal Chain: Normal cells carefully regulate growth and death through a network of proto-oncogenes and
tumor suppressors. Cancer often begins when this balance  is upset by mutations: proto-oncogenes
become constitutively active “oncogenes” (via gain-of-function mutations, gene amplification, or gene
fusions), while tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) are lost or silenced 171  172 . For example, a point mutation
in KRAS (e.g. G12D) locks it in an active GTP-bound state, sending continuous proliferative signals through
MAPK and PI3K pathways 171  172 . A fusion like BCR-ABL creates a constantly active tyrosine kinase driving
cell division in CML. On the TSG side, TP53 mutations or deletions disable the DNA damage checkpoint and
apoptosis trigger, and RB1 loss removes the G1/S cell cycle brake 173  174 . Typically, tumor initiation follows
the “multi-hit” model: an early oncogenic event might immortalize or give a clonal expansion (“initiating
seed”), then additional hits like TSG losses permit full malignant conversion 175  176 . The combined effect is
unchecked cell cycle entry (e.g. Cyclin E/CDK2 upregulated, RB disabled, E2F free) and evaded apoptosis
(e.g. upregulated BCL-2, loss of p53-mediated BAX) 177  178 . These are classic hallmarks achieved by
oncogene activation and TSG loss  20   21  . However, these drastic changes induce cellular stresses: strong
oncogenic signaling can cause replication stress and proteotoxic stress (excess protein production,
misfolding) 179  82  . For instance, MYC overexpression drives cells to produce ribosomes and proteins
beyond capacity, resulting in proteotoxic and oxidative stress; RAS activation pushes rapid growth leading to
metabolic and replication stress 179  82  . Cancer cells manage these stresses by heavily relying on certain
“support” pathways not themselves oncogenic – termed non-oncogene addictions  82   83  . For example,
RAS-driven cells become dependent on glutathione and other antioxidants to cope with high ROS (so they
need NRF2 pathway or high glutamine import); MYC-driven cells need robust chaperone systems like HSP90
to fold all the proteins being churned out  82   83  . These dependencies are like the Achilles’ heels created by
oncogene activation. If one inhibits HSP90 in a cell with many mutant oncoproteins, it preferentially kills
those cells because normal cells are not as stressed. This concept extends to synthetic lethal interactions:
e.g. loss of PTEN (a TSG) makes cells rely on the PI3K/AKT pathway in a specific way, or loss of ATM makes
cells reliant on ATR. Thus, the oncogene/TSG module both directly drives malignancy and indirectly creates
new vulnerabilities.

7. Tumor Microenvironment (TME) – Hypoxia, Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs),
Acidosis, Angiogenesis (Sink/Spread)

MVCL Role: Sink & Spread. The tumor microenvironment creates protective “sink” niches that shield cancer
cells from immune attack and therapeutic agents, while providing pro-survival signals 291  292 . Key features
include hypoxia, an acidic/high-lactate milieu, dense stromal cell presence  (like CAFs), and abnormal
vasculature. These niche conditions select for more aggressive phenotypes that can survive stress (a sink
function) and also facilitate the tumor’s local invasion and distant spread (via remodeling tissue structure
and preparing pre-metastatic niches) 293  294 . In essence, the TME not only nurtures tumor cells but
actively contributes to disease progression and metastasis.

Causal Chain: Rapid proliferation of tumor cells and disorganized angiogenesis lead to regions of hypoxia
and nutrient deprivation within the tumor 295  295 . Hypoxia stabilizes the transcription factor HIF-1α, which
triggers a cascade of responses: it upregulates VEGF to stimulate new blood vessel growth (though these
new vessels are often abnormal and leaky) 295  296 . HIF-1 also shifts cell metabolism to glycolysis (hence
lactate production) as described earlier 296 . Hypoxia (and signals from the tumor like TGF-β and cytokines)
activate resident fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) 297  298 . These CAFs proliferate and
secrete large amounts of extracellular matrix proteins like collagen, and enzymes like lysyl oxidase (LOX)
that cross-link collagen fibers 299  300 . The result is a dense, stiff stroma around the tumor. CAFs also
secrete cytokines/chemokines (TGF-β, IL-6, CXCL12, etc.) that directly promote tumor cell survival and
immunosuppression 299  300 . For example, TGF-β from CAFs induces EMT in cancer cells and also keeps
immune cells in check. The abnormal angiogenesis creates leaky, tortuous vessels – these leak fluid
causing high  interstitial pressure and heterogeneous perfusion 301  302 . Some tumor areas become
chronically under-perfused, reinforcing hypoxia in a vicious cycle (VEGF causes vessels, but they’re poor,
which causes more hypoxia). Accumulation of lactate and carbonic acid due to high glycolysis leads to
acidosis in the extracellular space 303  304 . Proton pumps and transporters (like CAIX and NHE1) on tumor
cells expel H^+ to maintain intracellular pH, further acidifying the microenvironment 257  305 . Low pH and
high adenosine (from ATP breakdown in hypoxia) suppress effector T cells and NK cells while attracting
immunosuppressive cell types (like M2 macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [MDSCs]) 306
 307 . In parallel, myeloid cells (monocytes, macrophages) are drawn in by tumor/CAF signals (CSF-1, CCL2,
etc.) and differentiate into tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which produce VEGF, EGF, TGF-β, etc.,
further reinforcing immunosuppression and aiding invasion. The ECM stiffening and high pressure from
collagen  cross-linking  and  fluid  buildup  physically  impedes  drug  delivery  and  also  triggers
mechanotransduction pathways (YAP/TAZ in cancer cells get activated by stiffness) which promote survival,
EMT, and therapy resistance 308  308 . LOX-mediated crosslinking of collagen not only stiffens tissue but
creates linear tracks along which cancer cells can migrate outward 309  310 . Altogether, the TME evolves into
a “fortress” protecting the tumor: immune cells are either kept out or deactivated, drugs have trouble
penetrating, and cancer cells are supported by stromal growth factors and matrix survival signals. This
environment effectively selects for clones that can survive low oxygen and low pH – those clones often have
more aggressive traits by necessity 311  311 . Meanwhile, the TME changes (like leaky vessels and matrix
remodeling) pave the way for tumor cells to invade surrounding tissue and eventually enter circulation
(facilitating spread).

8. Immune Evasion & Tumor Immunity (TIME) (Sink/Switch/Spread)

MVCL Role: Sink, Switch & Spread. The immune system can initially eliminate emerging tumor cells (an early
protective  “sink” function), but progressing cancers undergo immunoediting and develop immune
evasion mechanisms – effectively switching to an immune-resistant state 350  351 . This immune escape is
essential for a tumor to become a self-sustaining malignant system. Once immune evasion is established,
the tumor is free to spread (micrometastases survive in otherwise hostile new sites because they are not
eliminated by the immune system) 352  353 . In short, immune evasion is both a hallmark and an ongoing
dynamic: tumors co-evolve with the immune response, and ultimately successful tumors create an
immunosuppressive microenvironment as part of the malignant loop.

Causal Chain: In the early phase of tumor development, transformed cells express neoantigens – novel
peptides (often from mutations or aberrant expression) that the immune system can recognize as foreign

immune system mounts an antitumor response, primarily through cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NK cells that
can destroy highly immunogenic tumor cells – this is the elimination phase of cancer immunoediting 356
 357 . If not all tumor cells are eradicated, an equilibrium phase ensues: the immune system continues to
apply pressure, and tumor cell variants that are less visible or more resistant survive (this can last years in
dormant lesions) 358  359 . Eventually, escape occurs: the tumor selects for clones that avoid immune
destruction 360  361 . Mechanisms include: upregulation of immune checkpoints (e.g. PD-L1 on tumor
cells binds PD-1 on T  cells, turning them off) 361  362 ; loss of antigen presentation (mutations or
downregulation in MHC class I or β2-microglobulin so T cells cannot see the tumor antigens) 361  363 ;

destruction 388  389 . At the same time, therapy-induced senescence or inflammatory forms of cell death
contribute to a tumor-promoting microenvironment (sink) via chronic inflammation (the SASP), which can
nurture residual cancer cells 390  391 . In other words, cancer cells’ ability to avoid cell death (apoptotic
switch off) and to manipulate cell death programs (sometimes entering senescence or alternative death

Causal Chain: Normal cells undergoing excessive DNA damage or oncogenic stress will activate apoptosis
or permanent senescence as a fail-safe. In early tumorigenesis, cells acquire alterations that disable these
outcomes. The most common is loss of TP53 function, removing a key trigger of apoptosis and senescence
 392  393 . Additionally, cancers often upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL1) and
downregulate pro-apoptotics (BAX, BAK, or BH3-only proteins) 394  395 . This shift allows them to survive
conditions that would normally cause cell death, such as severe DNA damage, oncogene overexpression, or
detachment from the extracellular matrix (avoiding anoikis) 396  397 . With apoptosis blocked, cells become
susceptible to alternative regulated cell death (RCD) pathways if triggered. For example, a cell that won't
die by apoptosis might  still undergo necroptosis  if death receptor signaling occurs without caspase-8
activation (TNF-α -> RIPK1/RIPK3 -> MLKL pore formation) 398  399 . Necroptosis causes the cell to swell and
rupture, spilling contents and provoking inflammation (as  it’s a  lytic, immunogenic death). Similarly,
pyroptosis can be triggered by inflammasome activation (caspase-1 cleavage of gasdermin D, or caspase-3
cleavage of gasdermin E under certain conditions), causing an inflammatory cell explosion  56  400 . These
non-apoptotic deaths can kill cancer cells that have shut off apoptosis – effectively alternate kill switches.
However, they are double-edged: necroptosis or pyroptosis in tumors releases many neoantigens and
cytokines, which can recruit immune cells and potentially attack the tumor (converting a “cold” tumor to
“hot”) 401  402 . There's evidence that inducing pyroptosis in an immune-cold tumor causes a strong anti-
tumor immune response. Another RCD is ferroptosis – an iron-dependent form of cell death caused by
unchecked lipid peroxidation (often when cystine import or glutathione peroxidase [GPX4] is compromised)
 403  404 . Mesenchymal, therapy-resistant cancer  cells seem  particularly prone to ferroptosis  if not
protected by GPX4. Ferroptosis can kill cells that evade other deaths and is being actively researched as a
therapeutic induction target. Meanwhile, under sub-lethal stress (like chemo at low dose, or radiation),
tumor cells may enter senescence instead of dying 405  406 . Senescent tumor cells irreversibly stop
proliferating but remain metabolically active and secrete the senescence-associated secretory phenotype
(SASP) – a cocktail of pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and proteases (IL-6, IL-8, VEGF, etc.) 407
 408 . While senescence initially seems beneficial (the cell is no longer dividing), the SASP can paradoxically
promote tumor regrowth by stimulating neighboring cells, inducing EMT, recruiting suppressive immune
cells or angiogenesis 409  410 .  It  essentially makes the microenvironment more inflammatory and
supportive of malignant phenotypes. If those senescent cells persist (as a reservoir of tumor cells), they can
later be “reawakened” or replaced by new aggressive clones. Interestingly, one approach is to use senolytic
drugs (that selectively kill senescent cells) after therapy to eliminate these SASP producers 411  412 . Overall,
the causal chain is: early cancer disables apoptosis → cancer cells survive lethal triggers → alternative death
pathways become relevant (necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis) → these can cause inflammation  if
engaged → chronic sublethal stress leads to senescence → senescent cells promote a tumor-friendly,
inflamed niche (which ironically can help some tumor cells or tumor relapse). The interplay between cell
death and immune system is tight: immunogenic cell deaths can alert immunity, while immunosuppression
can let slightly injured cells avoid being cleared by immune phagocytes (so they linger as senescent,
secreting SASP).

family anti-apoptotics, and p53 is mutated in >50% of cancers (in others, p53 pathway often inactivated by
alternate means)  60   61  . Therefore, the principle that “cancer cells don’t die when they should” is invariant.

Causal Chain: The metastatic cascade starts with cancer cells in the primary tumor gaining invasive
capabilities. Often, a subset of tumor cells undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a
transcriptional program triggered by factors like TGF-β, TWIST, and SNAIL that converts polygonal epithelial
cells into motile, spindle-shaped mesenchymal cells 158  436 . During EMT (which can be partial/incomplete

metalloproteinases (MMPs) that carve paths through the tissue 309  310 . Using these pathways, tumor cells
invade locally through the basement membrane into surrounding stroma 437  437 . They may move as
single cells (especially if EMT is full) or as clusters (if partial EMT, maintaining some cell-cell junctions) 439

tumor vasculature and by tumor-associated macrophages that produce EGF signals and others to help
tumor cells enter vessels 441  442 . Once in circulation, these circulating tumor cells (CTCs) face shear
stress and immune attack (particularly by NK cells). Platelets often coat CTCs, protecting them from shear
and immune recognition 443  444 . Neutrophils can also escort CTCs (forming cell clusters) to help them
survive and lodge in capillaries 443  445 . CTCs then arrest in capillaries of distant organs, either by size
restriction or via adhesion molecule interactions. Extravasation (exit from vessels) is aided by the “pre-
metastatic niche” that was prepared: e.g. primary tumor-secreted factors cause target organ endothelia to
upregulate adhesion molecules and become permeable, and recruit myeloid cells that secrete proteases to

Evolutionary and Ecosystem Perspective: The MVCL underscores that cancer is not just a collection of
mutations but an evolving system with ecological interactions (cancer cells co-evolve with stromal cells and
immune cells in their microenvironment). Each loop cycle – from seed mutation to niche formation to
malignant switch to expansion – can be seen as an “iteration” of selection. Over time, a tumor may loop
repeatedly, each time possibly gaining more aggressive features. Importantly, our interventions often
target the “spread” phase (e.g. chemo kills proliferating cells in spread) or a specific switch (e.g. hormonal
therapy reverses the “oncogene switch” of ER in breast cancer). The MVCL suggests that intervening earlier
in the loop could halt progression more effectively. For instance, could we target the “seed” phase by
increasing genomic stability (a daunting task, but perhaps anti-inflammatory lifestyle or certain drugs like
aspirin have been postulated to reduce mutation accumulation)? Or interrupt the “sink” phase by fibroblast

imposing a bottleneck, akin to a seasonal change in an ecosystem that only some variants survive. The loop
then continues with those survivors. This is why single-agent therapy rarely cures advanced cancer:  it
knocks the loop down to a smaller size, but the loop continues with resistant members. The MVCL suggests
that multi-pronged therapy or sequential adaptive therapy is needed to prevent the loop from closing
again. Strikingly, adaptive therapy (maintaining some sensitive cells to suppress resistant ones) explicitly
acknowledges the loop’s self-sustaining nature  1    2  . By not trying to break the loop completely (which
often triggers rapid resistance),  it instead manages the loop. This  is a paradigm shift: from “cancer
elimination” to “cancer containment” strategy  32   35  .

Conclusion: We have presented the Minimal Viable Cancer Loop as a holistic framework of cancer biology
that links molecular hallmarks across scales – from genomic mutations to systemic immunosuppression – in
a self-propagating cycle. This framework mirrors how cancers behave clinically: they start as local seeds and
eventually loop into widespread, therapy-resistant disease. By understanding cancer as an integrated loop,
we can identify combination treatment strategies aimed at breaking key links and thus collapsing the
malignant cycle. The progress of the past several years – such as therapies that re-engage the immune
system, target “undruggable” addictions, or  intelligently manage tumor evolution – provides strong
validation for many aspects of this model. Moving forward, the MVCL can serve as a unifying map to guide
research: every new therapy or discovery can be plotted onto this map to see how it impacts the loop. The
ultimate goal is to prevent the loop from completing even a single full revolution in patients (i.e. intercept
cancer at a premalignant stage), or, if the loop is already running, to disrupt it so extensively at multiple
points that it cannot sustain itself. Break the loop, and we break the cancer.
